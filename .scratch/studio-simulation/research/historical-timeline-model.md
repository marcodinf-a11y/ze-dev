# Historical timeline evidence for the studio simulation

Research for “Which well-supported milestones from 1950 onward most changed how computer games could be built, distributed, and sold?” Sources below are original company material, contemporary records, or museums' holdings of original artifacts. The [PC game article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PC_game) supplied by the user is a useful orientation, but its milestones need narrower interpretation: a 1950 exhibition machine was not a personal computer or a commercial game studio.

| Period / milestone | Evidence | Simulation implication (inference) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1950, *Bertie the Brain* | Canada's science and technology museum reproduces Rogers Majestic's contemporary account and identifies Kates's tic-tac-toe machine as a 1950 Canadian National Exhibition display to publicize Additron tubes. [Museum historical study and reproduced original material](https://publications.gc.ca/collections/collection_2018/mstc-cstm/NM33-1-17-eng.pdf) | Start with exhibition, sponsorship, research time, access to scarce hardware, and public attention. Ordinary unit sales, publishers, and a mass customer base should unlock later. |
| 1962, *Spacewar!* on PDP-1 | The Computer History Museum preserves/reconstructs original code and credits its 1962 builders. [Original code reconstruction](https://www.computerhistory.org/pdp-1/playspacewar/); [DEC's contemporary Spacewar demonstration text](https://archive.computerhistory.org/resources/access/text/2018/08/102740407-05-01-acc.pdf) | Institutional access and peer exchange are distinct from consumer distribution. A game's technical demands can drive skill, hardware, and reputation even when revenue is small or absent. |
| 1976–81, transferable software and home computers | The museum holds Crowther's 1976 *Adventure* source listing for a timesharing PDP-10; IBM describes 1981's open IBM PC architecture, games, third-party software, and new retail channels. [Adventure source artifact](https://www.computerhistory.org/revolution/artifact/190/786); [IBM PC history](https://www.ibm.com/history/personal-computer) | Model hardware families and portability as a development cost; consumer ownership and retail channels create an addressable market. The first personal-computer market should include more than the later IBM-compatible PC. |
| Early 1980s, removable media and software trade | IBM documents floppy disks' role in independent software, including 1977 Apple II use, 1981 IBM PC drives, and increasing capacity. [IBM floppy-disk history](https://www.ibm.com/history/floppy-disk) | Physical distribution needs manufacturing, inventory, shipping, shelf space, and returns. Storage capacity constrains game scope. |
| 1993–96, shareware, CD-ROM, Windows graphics | id Software's contemporary *DOOM* plan explicitly specified a shareware first episode; a 1994 computer trade paper describes *Myst*'s PC CD-ROM requirements; Microsoft's 1996 announcements describe DirectX and Direct3D adoption for Windows games. [id's DOOM plan/press release](https://gheja.github.io/game-design-documents/doombible.pdf); [1994 PC trade paper](https://archive.computerhistory.org/resources/access/text/2023/11/102803718-05-011-acc.pdf); [Microsoft Direct3D announcement](https://news.microsoft.com/source/1996/06/13/microsoft-ships-direct3d/) | Distribution can be free trial plus paid conversion as well as boxed retail. More storage and graphics APIs enable ambitious content while raising production cost and hardware compatibility risk. |
| 2003 onward, platform distribution and continuing service | Valve's 2003 Steam update archive and its own handbook show the emerging client/service and later third-party distribution; current Steamworks documentation shows coming-soon pages, wishlists, release process, and per-app fees. [Steam 2003 archive](https://store.steampowered.com/oldnews/207?l=english); [Valve handbook](https://media.steampowered.com/apps/valve/Valve_Handbook_LowRes.pdf); [Steamworks release options](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/store/types); [Steamworks wishlists](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/marketing/wishlist); [Steam Direct fee](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/gettingstarted/appfee) | Digital distribution changes costs and discovery rather than eliminating gatekeepers. Add store presence, pre-release interest, updates, and post-release support as decisions. Do not backdate today's Steam fees or rules to its launch. |
| 2015 onward, more flexible direct creator sales | itch.io states that it introduced configurable revenue sharing in March 2015 and supports pay-what-you-want pricing. [itch.io's own history and terms](https://itch.io/docs/general/about); [creator pricing documentation](https://itch.io/docs/creators/pricing) | Represent channels by changing terms and affordances, not one universal “digital store” modifier. |

## Recommendations for the timeline decision

1. **Separate historical anchors from mechanics.** Store dated events as data: an earliest plausible date or window, affected hardware/technology, channel, market, and sources. The simulation then updates availability and costs. Avoid using a single “era level” that makes all systems change together.
2. **Keep historical facts as baselines, not a fixed plot.** Platform adoption, competitors, and audience demand can respond to player actions within plausible bounds. This leaves the next decision—how much history the player may alter—open.
3. **Treat 1950–early 1970s as an unusual prologue.** The evidence supports exhibition and academic work, not a modern wage-funded, consumer-facing studio in 1950. If the player starts there, use patronage, lab access, or commissioned demonstrations until a retail software market develops.
4. **Model multiple overlapping channels and machines.** Mainframes, home micros, IBM compatibles, DOS/Windows, physical retail, shareware, and digital stores coexist for periods. Technology adoption should be gradual; dates mark availability, not an instant global replacement.
5. **Choose a playable end date separately.** These sources establish milestones, not a satisfying campaign length. Later decisions should set time step, scope of simulation, end condition, and whether later eras are expansions.

## Source limits

This is a selective list of mechanics-changing anchors, not a complete PC game history. The 1950 example is a game-playing exhibition computer, not a PC. Specific market sizes, costs, conversion rates, and review effects remain unsupported here and need separate economy research or fictional balancing assumptions.
